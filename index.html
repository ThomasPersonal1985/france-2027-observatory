THE SHORT VERSION

Elections for dummies

A plain-English guide for first-time voters and anyone who wants the basics without political jargon.

Beginner friendly

1. What is this election?

France is choosing its next president. Emmanuel Macron cannot run for a third consecutive term. The president is elected directly by voters, not chosen by parliament.

2. How does voting work?

It normally takes two rounds. In round one, voters choose from all the candidates. If nobody gets more than half of the valid votes, the two candidates with the most votes go to round two. Everyone votes again, and the candidate with more votes wins.

3. When is it?

The first round is scheduled for 18 April 2027 and the second round for 2 May 2027. Some overseas French territories vote the day before because of time zones.

4. Who is allowed to vote?

You must be a French citizen, at least 18 years old by the relevant date, and registered on the electoral roll. The ordinary registration deadline for this election is 12 March 2027. Special cases can have different rules.

5. Why do candidates need 500 signatures?

Before someone can appear on the presidential ballot, they need support from at least 500 elected officials, such as mayors and parliamentarians. These are called parrainages or sponsorships. They must come from at least 30 different departments or overseas territories, and no more than 50 can come from any one of them. A candidate saying “I have 500 promises” is not the same as having 500 signatures officially validated.

6. What does the president actually do?

The president represents France, appoints the prime minister, has an important role in foreign affairs and defence, can call certain elections, and signs laws. But the president does not govern alone. The government and parliament make laws and manage much of domestic policy. If the president and parliamentary majority come from opposing political camps, they must share power. This is called cohabitation.

7. What are the big issues in this campaign?

  • Cost of living: wages, prices, taxes and household budgets.
  • Pensions: when people can retire and how the system is paid for.
  • Immigration: who can come to France, residency rules and integration.
  • Security: policing, crime and the justice system.
  • Climate and energy: nuclear power, renewables, transport and emissions.
  • Europe and the world: France’s role in the EU, NATO, Ukraine and international conflicts.
  • Public services: schools, hospitals, housing and transport.

8. How do I understand the candidates?

Start with what each candidate has actually promised. A party’s old manifesto, a politician’s past job and a new campaign promise are three different things. This observatory labels the evidence and links to the original reporting or documents.

9. What do opinion polls tell us?

A poll asks a sample of people how they might vote. It is a snapshot, not a prediction. Polls can change, and results depend on who is included in the question, how it is asked and when it is asked. A candidate missing from a poll was not necessarily polling at zero.

10. What happens next?

Campaigns continue, candidates seek sponsorships, the Constitutional Council publishes the final list, and the official campaign period begins. The final ballot list is not settled until the official process is complete.

The guide explains the process, not who anyone should vote for. Dates and rules are based on the French government’s published election calendar and may be updated by official decree.

TRANSPARENCY

Sources & methodology

Every factual claim should be traceable to its source and date.

Evidence labels

  • Official: government, Constitutional Council, candidate's own programme or party document.
  • Direct statement: speech, debate, interview or social post, with date and context.
  • News report: journalistic account, attributed to the publisher and journalist where available.
  • Analysis: interpretation, kept separate from the candidate's own words.
  • Unverified: not used as an established factual claim.

Polling rules

  • Store pollster, sponsor, fieldwork dates, sample, method, question wording and candidate scenario.
  • Do not treat a tracker snapshot as an individual poll.
  • Do not treat a polling average as a poll.
  • Missing candidates are null, never zero.
